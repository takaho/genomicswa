We identified four distinct multimorbidity patterns among older adults with CHD and showed that these patterns were differentially associated with unintentional falls and fractures. The patterns point to the interaction between cardiovascular, metabolic, musculoskeletal and neurological systems in fall risk.

In 189,558 CHD patients, multimorbidity was common and strongly associated with fall risk. Hypertension, heart failure, diabetes, dyslipidemia and cerebrovascular diseases were the most frequent comorbidities, consistent with prior studies ( [REF] ). Previous studies have reported variable multimorbidity patterns among individuals with multiple cardiovascular diseases ( [REF] ). Earlier analyses were limited to 13–15 conditions ( [REF] ); we evaluated 31 chronic conditions. In addition to the known cardiovascular-metabolic clusters, we found new patterns linking CHD with gastrointestinal and osteoarticular disorders. These patterns carried different fall risks, and the cerebrovascular-osteoarticular complex pattern had the highest risks of unintentional falls and of fractures after falls, particularly among women.

While we labeled the latent classes by their most prevalent conditions, each class showed considerable internal heterogeneity (Supplementary Table S3). For example, in the "gastrointestinal-dominant" class, cerebral infarction introduces possible motor impairment and cognitive deficit, which adds to the baseline fall risk ( [REF] ). Similarly, COPD in the "core cardiovascular disease" class may worsen functional decline through reduced exercise tolerance and muscle weakness ( [REF] ). This complexity means that a uniform intervention strategy based only on the dominant condition would be inadequate. A stratified approach is needed instead: initial management should target the defining condition, but should be followed by systematic screening for high-risk comorbidities within that cluster. For example, patients in the "cerebrovascular-osteoarticular complex" class should be assessed not only for balance and gait deficits but also for dyslipidemia and diabetes, which can contribute to peripheral neuropathy and further impair stability ( [REF] ). Recognizing this heterogeneity within clusters is thus important for designing interventions that address the full range of an individual's fall risk.
