# Structural Biology: research-article prose style, 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Structural Biology article of 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Structural Biology articles (2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.
- Targets are the averages of papers in this field whose last author worked in an English-speaking country (US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland); "all fields" is the average of all 2019–2022 papers.
- Never add a word of contrast or cause (whereas, while, although, but, however, because, since, thus, therefore, hence, so, consequently) that the original does not have: it changes the argument.
- Keep every hedge (may, might, could, likely, suggest, appear, potential) and every negation (not, no, without): their number must not change.
- When joining sentences, use "and", "which" or a semicolon, and only when the second sentence refers back to the first (It, They, This …).

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Structural Biology articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.3 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Do not add "therefore", "thus" or "because": English-speaking authors used fewer causal connectives than other authors; keep only those already in the original.
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- English-speaking authors used the passive less than other authors: keep existing passives, but write what the authors did with "we" ("We measured X", not "X was measured by us").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Structural Biology introduction targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.6 per 300 words; importance words about 0.7 per 300 words; nominalisations about 17.7 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.6 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.53 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Structural Biology discussion targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.7 per 300 words; importance words about 0.6 per 300 words; nominalisations about 18.6 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.2 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.41 characters per word.

## Style illustration (Structural Biology, 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants); verbatim, CC BY; do not copy content)
- Introduction (PMC9768166, Nature Communications, 2022): "In the last few years, cryo-ET has become the state-of-the-art technique for visualizing protein complexes in the context of the native whole cell at high resolution. Unlike traditional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) staining techniques, cryo-ET enables imaging of cellular structures in a native cellular environment, maintaining their three-dimensional nanoarchitecture while fully hydrated . In cryo-ET the sample is vitrified without staining, a tilt series of images from a multitude of angles is taken, and a three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the sample is calculated from the tilt series at nanometer resolution."
- Discussion (PMC9467914, Nature Methods, 2022): "Using iDPC–STEM, we imaged plunge-frozen KLH and TMV as biological test specimens to assess the image quality and analyzed the resulting 3D cryo-EM density maps. We demonstrated that iDPC–STEM produces high-contrast in-focus micrographs of ice-embedded molecular KLH particles and the regular helical image features of TMV. When we subjected multiple micrographs to the single-particle reconstruction workflow, we determined the subnanometer KLH structure at 6.5 Å and several near-atomic resolution TMV structures using different iDPC–STEM imaging conditions down to 3.5 Å."
