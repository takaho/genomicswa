# Pharmacy: research-article prose style, 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Pharmacy article of 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Pharmacy articles (2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.
- Targets are the averages of papers in this field whose last author worked in an English-speaking country (US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland); "all fields" is the average of all 2019–2022 papers.
- Never add a word of contrast or cause (whereas, while, although, but, however, because, since, thus, therefore, hence, so, consequently) that the original does not have: it changes the argument.
- Keep every hedge (may, might, could, likely, suggest, appear, potential) and every negation (not, no, without): their number must not change.
- When joining sentences, use "and", "which" or a semicolon, and only when the second sentence refers back to the first (It, They, This …).

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive.
- **Passive voice: less than other fields** — target about 2.0 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: write what the authors themselves did with "we" as the subject; never in sentences that report earlier work ([REF]), and keep passives whose agent is unknown or other researchers.
- **Word length: more than other fields** — target about 5.77 characters per word (all fields about 5.66 characters per word). How: prefer precise technical and Latinate terms and noun phrases. Example: "X goes up when Y is blocked" → "X increases upon inhibition of Y". Apply in several places.
- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): more than other fields** — target about 21.4 per 300 words (all fields about 19.0 per 300 words). How: express processes as nouns ("the activation of X", "expression", "regulation"). Example: "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow" → "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation". Apply in several places.
- **Past tense: more than other fields** — target about 2.6 in 10 finite verbs (all fields about 2.3 in 10 finite verbs). How: report earlier studies and this study's observations in the past tense. Example: "Previous studies show that X regulates Y" → "Previous studies showed that X regulated Y". Apply in several places.
- **Sentence length: more than other fields** — target about 26 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: join a short sentence with the next one only when the second refers back to the first, using "which" or "and"; never add whereas/because. Example: "X was measured. It increased with age." → "X, which was measured in all samples, increased with age.". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Pharmacy; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Pharmacy; aim statements is more common in Pharmacy. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **This/these/they as subject: more than other fields** — target about 2.1 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: refer back with this/these/they/which as subjects. Example: "X binds Y. The binding of X to Y…" → "X binds Y. This…". Apply in several places.
- **Word length: more than other fields** — target about 5.66 characters per word (all fields about 5.49 characters per word). How: prefer precise technical and Latinate terms and noun phrases. Example: "X goes up when Y is blocked" → "X increases upon inhibition of Y". Apply in several places.
- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive. Example: "We found that X increased" → "X increased". Apply in several places.
- **Booster verbs: less than other fields** — target about 1.1 per 300 words (all fields about 1.7 per 300 words). How: prefer plain "show" or "find" to "demonstrate", "reveal", "highlight", "underscore"; do not replace them with hedges such as "suggest" or "indicate". Example: "These results reveal that X…" → "These results show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Vocabulary variety: more than other fields** — target MATTR about 0.70 (all fields MATTR about 0.69). How: vary wording; avoid repeating the same noun or verb in neighbouring sentences. Example: avoid repeating "increase" in neighbouring sentences: "rise", "elevation", "higher levels". Apply in several places.
- **Passive voice: less than other fields** — target about 2.0 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.3 in 10 clause subjects). How: write what the authors themselves did with "we" as the subject; never in sentences that report earlier work ([REF]), and keep passives whose agent is unknown or other researchers. Example: "Samples were collected from 12 sites" → "We collected samples from 12 sites". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Pharmacy; limitation statements is more common in Pharmacy; future-work statements is more common in Pharmacy. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Pharmacy articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.4 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Do not add "therefore", "thus" or "because": English-speaking authors used fewer causal connectives than other authors; keep only those already in the original.
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- English-speaking authors used the passive less than other authors: keep existing passives, but write what the authors did with "we" ("We measured X", not "X was measured by us").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Pharmacy introduction targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.6 per 300 words; importance words about 0.9 per 300 words; nominalisations about 21.4 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.0 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.77 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Pharmacy discussion targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.7 per 300 words; importance words about 0.9 per 300 words; nominalisations about 20.3 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 1.0 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.0 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.66 characters per word.
