# Drug Discovery: research-article prose style, 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Drug Discovery article of 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Drug Discovery articles (2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.
- Targets are the averages of papers in this field whose last author worked in an English-speaking country (US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland); "all fields" is the average of all 2019–2022 papers.
- Never add a word of contrast or cause (whereas, while, although, but, however, because, since, thus, therefore, hence, so, consequently) that the original does not have: it changes the argument.
- Keep every hedge (may, might, could, likely, suggest, appear, potential) and every negation (not, no, without): their number must not change.
- When joining sentences, use "and", "which" or a semicolon, and only when the second sentence refers back to the first (It, They, This …).

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Drug Discovery articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.4 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Do not add "therefore", "thus" or "because": English-speaking authors used fewer causal connectives than other authors; keep only those already in the original.
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- English-speaking authors used the passive less than other authors: keep existing passives, but write what the authors did with "we" ("We measured X", not "X was measured by us").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Drug Discovery introduction targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.7 per 300 words; importance words about 0.8 per 300 words; nominalisations about 16.5 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.6 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.64 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Drug Discovery discussion targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.6 per 300 words; importance words about 0.8 per 300 words; nominalisations about 17.4 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 1.1 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.9 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.4 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.53 characters per word.

## Style illustration (Drug Discovery, 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants); verbatim, CC BY; do not copy content)
- Introduction (PMC6803698, Scientific Reports, 2019): "In this study, we combine homology modeling, molecular docking, and Quantum Mechanics-Molecular Mechanics (QM-MM) to define a binding hypothesis for the halogenated phenylalanine-based ligands of LAT1. In the first step, compounds were docked to a homology model of human LAT1, which was followed by the assessment of the docking poses using a common scaffold clustering technique to identify a common binding mode. Subsequently, selected docking poses were optimized through hybrid QM-MM method to calculate the interaction energy of the halogen-bonded adducts."
- Discussion (PMC6929327, BMC Bioinformatics, 2019): "In this work, we propose a novel positive-unlabeled learning method named DDI-PULearn for large-scale drug-drug interaction predictions. DDI-PULearn first generates seeds of reliable negative samples from the unlabeled samples using two techniques namely OCSVM and KNN. Then trained with the generated seeds, DDI-PULearn employs SVM to identify all reliable negative samples iteratively. Following that, DDI-PULearn represents the labeled positive samples and identified negative samples as vectors by a similarity-based representation method using abundant drug properties. Finally, the vectors are compressed via PCA and further used as input for binary classifications."
