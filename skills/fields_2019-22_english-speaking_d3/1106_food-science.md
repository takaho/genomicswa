# Food Science: research-article prose style, 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Food Science article of 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Food Science articles (2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.
- Targets are the averages of papers in this field whose last author worked in an English-speaking country (US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland); "all fields" is the average of all 2019–2022 papers.
- Never add a word of contrast or cause (whereas, while, although, but, however, because, since, thus, therefore, hence, so, consequently) that the original does not have: it changes the argument.
- Keep every hedge (may, might, could, likely, suggest, appear, potential) and every negation (not, no, without): their number must not change.
- When joining sentences, use "and", "which" or a semicolon, and only when the second sentence refers back to the first (It, They, This …).

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Word length: more than other fields** — target about 5.72 characters per word (all fields about 5.66 characters per word). How: prefer precise technical and Latinate terms and noun phrases. Example: "X goes up when Y is blocked" → "X increases upon inhibition of Y". Apply in several places.
- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): less than other fields** — target about 17.9 per 300 words (all fields about 19.0 per 300 words). How: express processes as verbs ("X activates Y"). Example: "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation" → "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is more common in Food Science; patients/participants as subject is less common in Food Science. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Food Science (use where it fits): "In recent …"; "It was …"; "The essential …"; "Most of …"; "In such …"; "In particular …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Word length: more than other fields** — target about 5.58 characters per word (all fields about 5.49 characters per word). How: prefer precise technical and Latinate terms and noun phrases. Example: "X goes up when Y is blocked" → "X increases upon inhibition of Y". Apply in several places.
- **Sentence length: more than other fields** — target about 27 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: join a short sentence with the next one only when the second refers back to the first, using "which" or "and"; never add whereas/because. Example: "X was measured. It increased with age." → "X, which was measured in all samples, increased with age.". Apply in several places.
- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: more than other fields** — target about 5.9 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 5.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: make genes, proteins, cells, compounds, diseases or variables the grammatical subjects. Example: "We observed that X binds Y" → "X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Importance words: more than other fields** — target about 0.9 per 300 words (all fields about 0.7 per 300 words). How: use more of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major. Example: "X regulates Y" → "X is a key regulator of Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is more common in Food Science; patients/participants as subject is less common in Food Science; aim statements is more common in Food Science. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Food Science (use where it fits): "On the …"; "It was …"; "The addition …"; "All the …"; "In recent …"; "The essential …"

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Food Science articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.3 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Do not add "therefore", "thus" or "because": English-speaking authors used fewer causal connectives than other authors; keep only those already in the original.
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- English-speaking authors used the passive less than other authors: keep existing passives, but write what the authors did with "we" ("We measured X", not "X was measured by us").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Food Science introduction targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.8 per 300 words; importance words about 1.1 per 300 words; nominalisations about 17.9 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.6 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.72 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Food Science discussion targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.6 per 300 words; importance words about 0.9 per 300 words; nominalisations about 19.6 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 1.1 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.3 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.58 characters per word.
