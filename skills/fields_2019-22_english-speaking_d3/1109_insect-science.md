# Insect Science: research-article prose style, 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Insect Science article of 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Insect Science articles (2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.
- Targets are the averages of papers in this field whose last author worked in an English-speaking country (US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland); "all fields" is the average of all 2019–2022 papers.
- Never add a word of contrast or cause (whereas, while, although, but, however, because, since, thus, therefore, hence, so, consequently) that the original does not have: it changes the argument.
- Keep every hedge (may, might, could, likely, suggest, appear, potential) and every negation (not, no, without): their number must not change.
- When joining sentences, use "and", "which" or a semicolon, and only when the second sentence refers back to the first (It, They, This …).

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): less than other fields** — target about 16.8 per 300 words (all fields about 19.0 per 300 words). How: express processes as verbs ("X activates Y"). Example: "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation" → "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow". Apply in several places.
- **Findings/data/study as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects). How: make the entity or "we" the subject instead of data/results/study.
- **Vocabulary variety: more than other fields** — target MATTR about 0.72 (all fields MATTR about 0.70). How: vary wording; avoid repeating the same noun or verb in neighbouring sentences. Example: avoid repeating "increase" in neighbouring sentences: "rise", "elevation", "higher levels". Apply in several places.
- **Passive voice: less than other fields** — target about 2.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: write what the authors themselves did with "we" as the subject; never in sentences that report earlier work ([REF]), and keep passives whose agent is unknown or other researchers. - **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…"). Example: "These results show that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Sentence length: more than other fields** — target about 26 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: join a short sentence with the next one only when the second refers back to the first, using "which" or "and"; never add whereas/because. Example: "X was measured. - Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is more common in Insect Science; patients/participants as subject is less common in Insect Science. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Insect Science articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.3 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Do not add "therefore", "thus" or "because": English-speaking authors used fewer causal connectives than other authors; keep only those already in the original.
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- English-speaking authors used the passive less than other authors: keep existing passives, but write what the authors did with "we" ("We measured X", not "X was measured by us").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Insect Science introduction targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.9 per 300 words; importance words about 0.9 per 300 words; nominalisations about 16.8 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.2 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.64 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Insect Science discussion targets (English-speaking authors) for these habits: causal connectives about 0.8 per 300 words; importance words about 0.7 per 300 words; nominalisations about 17.7 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.9 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.0 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.52 characters per word.

## Style illustration (Insect Science, 2019–2022, English-speaking authors (before AI writing assistants); verbatim, CC BY; do not copy content)

This research is motivated by the findings that certain midgut bacterial species can enhance or suppress viral and parasitic infections in mosquitoes [ ] and that midgut bacterial symbionts could be genetically-modified to express anti-pathogen molecules that reduce vector competence (paratransgenesis) [ ]."
- Discussion (PMC7724791, BMC Plant Biology, 2020): "We combined a number of methods, lead extracts, cumulative headspace sampling and real-time headspace sampling using a multi-cuvette system [ ], to investigate the response of five different tansy chemotypes to attack by a sucking and a chewing herbivore, and priming effects. Aphid feeding did not result in strong chemical changes, which might be related to a minimum infestation threshold of feeding aphids [ ], whereas caterpillar feeding increased stored and emitted volatiles across each plant chemotype. Importantly, there were chemotype-specific responses to herbivore attack."
