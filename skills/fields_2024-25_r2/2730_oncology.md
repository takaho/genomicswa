# Oncology: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Oncology article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Oncology articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Summary connectives: more than other fields** — target about 0.3 per 300 words (all fields about 0.2 per 300 words). How: use more of: together, collectively, taken together, overall, in summary, in conclusion. Example: "…" → "Taken together, …". Apply in several places.
- **Contrast connectives: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.2 per 300 words). How: use more of: however, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless, despite.
- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…"). Example: "These results show that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.3 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight. Example: "We found that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): gap/novelty statements is more common in Oncology; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Oncology. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Oncology (use where it fits): "These results …"; "We previously …"; "Our findings …"; "Nevertheless the …"; "Recent studies …"; "In this …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Summary connectives: more than other fields** — target about 0.4 per 300 words (all fields about 0.3 per 300 words). How: use more of: together, collectively, taken together, overall, in summary, in conclusion. Example: "…" → "Taken together, …". Apply in several places.
- **Contrast connectives: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use more of: however, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless, despite.

Typical discussion phrasing in Oncology (use where it fits): "Interestingly in …"; "In summary …"; "First this …"; "In our …"; "In addition …"; "Moreover we …"

## Vocabulary
- Words often added by AI polishing (additionally, notably, crucial, enhance, comprehensive, insights, robust, enhancing, enhances, elucidate) occur about 1.0 times per 300 words in Oncology; do not exceed that, and do not add them where the original has none.
