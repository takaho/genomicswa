# Microbiology: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Microbiology article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Microbiology articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Modal hedges: less than other fields** — target about 0.7 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: may, might, could, would. Example: "X may explain Y" → "X explains Y" (only where the excerpt states it as a finding). Apply in several places.
- **Causal connectives: less than other fields** — target about 0.6 per 300 words (all fields about 0.7 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: therefore, thus, hence, consequently, because, as a result. Do not overshoot: stop at the target (at most about 1 per 300 words).
- Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is more common in Microbiology. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Microbiology (use where it fits): "The most …"; "We have …"; "Most of …"; "We also …"; "However to …"; "In our …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Findings/data/study as subject: less than other fields** — target about 1.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.3 in 10 clause subjects). How: make the entity or "we" the subject instead of data/results/study. Example: "These data indicate that X binds Y" → "X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is more common in Microbiology; limitation statements is less common in Microbiology; patients/participants as subject is less common in Microbiology. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Microbiology (use where it fits): "A previous …"; "From to …"; "We previously …"; "Future work …"; "We were …"; "However a …"

## Vocabulary
- Words often added by AI polishing (additionally, notably, crucial, comprehensive, insights, enhance, robust, enhancing, enhances, underscores) occur about 0.8 times per 300 words in Microbiology; do not exceed that, and do not add them where the original has none.

- Introduction (PMC11279943, Pharmaceutics, 2024): "Approximately 80% of all microbial infections in the human body involve the formation of multicellular communities called biofilms [ ]. Bacterial biofilms formed on the surface of medical devices make the infection inherently more difficult to treat [ ]. Bacteria in a biofilm secrete an extracellular polymeric substance (EPS), which encases them in a protective matrix. This matrix makes biofilms extremely difficult to eliminate and leads to decreased susceptibility to antibiotics [ ]."
