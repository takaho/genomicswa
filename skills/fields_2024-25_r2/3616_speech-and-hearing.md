# Speech and Hearing: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Speech and Hearing article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Speech and Hearing articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Modal hedges: more than other fields** — target about 1.3 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: may, might, could, would. Do not overshoot: stop at the target (at most about 2 per 300 words).
- **Vocabulary variety: less than other fields** — target MATTR about 0.71 (all fields MATTR about 0.72). How: repeat key terms consistently instead of synonyms. Example: keep one term for one concept: do not alternate "rise", "elevation", "increase". Apply in several places.
- **Importance words: less than other fields** — target about 0.8 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major.
- **Hedging verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.0 per 300 words (all fields about 0.8 per 300 words). How: use more of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose. Example: "X causes Y" → "These data suggest that X causes Y". Apply in several places.
- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.3 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive. Example: "We found that X increased" → "X increased". Apply in several places.
- **Booster verbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.9 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight. Example: "These results highlight/reveal that X…" → "These results indicate that X…". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Speech and Hearing; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Speech and Hearing. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Speech and Hearing (use where it fits): "The aim …"; "As such …"; "However no …"; "There is …"; "In a …"; "However to …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 5.7 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity. Example: "X binds Y" → "We found that X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **"It is…"/"There are…" constructions: more than other fields** — target about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: use "It is … that" and "There is/are …" constructions. Example: "X is known to regulate Y" → "It is known that X regulates Y". Apply in several places.
- **Modal hedges: more than other fields** — target about 2.3 per 300 words (all fields about 2.0 per 300 words). How: use more of: may, might, could, would.
- **Importance words: less than other fields** — target about 0.7 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major.
- **Booster verbs: less than other fields** — target about 1.6 per 300 words (all fields about 1.9 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight. Example: "These results highlight/reveal that X…" → "These results indicate that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Past tense: more than other fields** — target about 4.5 in 10 finite verbs (all fields about 4.1 in 10 finite verbs). How: report earlier studies and this study's observations in the past tense. Example: "Previous studies show that X regulates Y" → "Previous studies showed that X regulated Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Speech and Hearing; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Speech and Hearing; future-work statements is more common in Speech and Hearing. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Speech and Hearing (use where it fits): "There was …"; "Further research …"; "That is …"; "They found …"; "This can …"; "There are …"
