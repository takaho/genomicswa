# Surgery: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Surgery article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Surgery articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Additive connectives: less than other fields** — target about 1.0 per 300 words (all fields about 1.2 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: moreover, furthermore, in addition, also, similarly, likewise. Example: "Moreover, X…" → "X…". Apply in several places.
- **Contrast connectives: more than other fields** — target about 1.4 per 300 words (all fields about 1.2 per 300 words). How: use more of: however, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless, despite.
- **Hedging adverbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.0 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often. Example: "X is involved" → "X is likely involved". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): method/model/approach as subject is more common in Surgery. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Surgery (use where it fits): "In our …"; "The aim …"; "This study …"; "Despite the …"; "Therefore we …"; "Therefore the …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Sentence length: less than other fields** — target about 24 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: split long sentences; one idea per sentence. Example: "X, which was measured in all samples and which increased with age, was associated with Y." → "X was measured in all samples. It increased with age and was associated with Y.". Apply in several places.
- **Contrast connectives: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use more of: however, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless, despite. Example: "X… Y…" → "X…; however, Y…". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging verbs: less than other fields** — target about 1.1 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose. Example: "This suggests that X causes Y" → "X causes Y" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): limitation statements is more common in Surgery; patients/participants as subject is more common in Surgery; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Surgery. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Surgery (use where it fits): "In our …"; "There was …"; "They found …"; "In such …"; "Several studies …"; "In their …"

## Vocabulary
- Words often added by AI polishing (additionally, notably, crucial, comprehensive, enhance, insights, enhancing, robust, enhances, elucidate) occur about 0.9 times per 300 words in Surgery; do not exceed that, and do not add them where the original has none.
