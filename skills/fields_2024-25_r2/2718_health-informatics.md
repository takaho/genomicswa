# Health Informatics: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Health Informatics article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Health Informatics articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Passive voice: less than other fields** — target about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: turn passives into active clauses with an explicit agent. Example: "X was measured in all samples" → "We measured X in all samples". Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity.
- **Vocabulary variety: more than other fields** — target MATTR about 0.74 (all fields MATTR about 0.72). How: vary wording; avoid repeating the same noun or verb in neighbouring sentences. Example: avoid repeating "increase" in neighbouring sentences: "rise", "elevation", "higher levels". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging verbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.6 per 300 words (all fields about 0.8 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose. Example: "This suggests that X causes Y" → "X causes Y" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- **Causal connectives: less than other fields** — target about 0.6 per 300 words (all fields about 0.7 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: therefore, thus, hence, consequently, because, as a result. Do not overshoot: stop at the target (at most about 1 per 300 words).
- **Word length: more than other fields** — target about 5.89 characters per word (all fields about 5.81 characters per word). How: prefer precise technical and Latinate terms and noun phrases. Example: "X goes up when Y is blocked" → "X increases upon inhibition of Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): method/model/approach as subject is more common in Health Informatics; limitation statements is more common in Health Informatics; aim statements is more common in Health Informatics. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Health Informatics (use where it fits): "This study …"; "While these …"; "Through this …"; "In other …"; "The recent …"; "Specifically we …"

## Style illustration (Health Informatics, 2024–2025; verbatim, CC BY; do not copy content)
- Introduction (PMC11108146, PLoS ONE, 2024): "Manually curating genomic repositories and annotating genetic relationships from literature is time-consuming and challenging [ ]. Traditional reading and summarising lack scalability, are subject to personal interpretations, and are time-consuming. Automating the pre-processing of scientific literature and post-processing of extracted information using Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques can address these limitations. Several researchers have studied automating pre-processing and post-processing for accurately extracting relations from unstructured data. Most pre-processing techniques involve the extraction of all available abstracts within a specific timeline from online resources such as PubMed [ ]."
- Discussion (PMC12023801, JMIR Medical Informatics, 2025): "The timing of AI advice relative to the user’s decision may influence the user’s performance and trust. Gajos and Mamykina [ ] recently examined AI advice timing in 3 conditions: (1) AI recommendation and explanation before making a decision, (2) AI recommendation and explanation after making a decision, and (3) AI explanation only before making a decision. While all 3 conditions led to improved decision accuracy, only the third condition showed improvement in both decision accuracy and learning gain."
