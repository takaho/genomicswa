# Neurology: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Neurology article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Neurology articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.7 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight.
- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…"). Example: "These results show that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.0 per 300 words (all fields about 0.8 per 300 words). How: use more of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose.
- **Word length: more than other fields** — target about 5.90 characters per word (all fields about 5.81 characters per word). How: prefer precise technical and Latinate terms and noun phrases. Example: "X goes up when Y is blocked" → "X increases upon inhibition of Y". Apply in several places.
- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): more than other fields** — target about 21.8 per 300 words (all fields about 20.2 per 300 words). How: express processes as nouns ("the activation of X", "expression", "regulation"). Example: "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow" → "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation". Apply in several places.
- **Findings/data/study as subject: more than other fields** — target about 1.0 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects). How: make findings/data/results/this study the subject ("These findings suggest…"). Example: "X increased, suggesting…" → "These findings suggest that X…". Apply in several places.

Typical introduction phrasing in Neurology (use where it fits): "Taken together …"; "Previous research …"; "Recent research …"; "Our results …"; "These findings …"; "Our findings …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Hedging verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use more of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose.
- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 2.1 per 300 words (all fields about 1.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight. Example: "We found that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): more than other fields** — target about 21.7 per 300 words (all fields about 20.4 per 300 words). How: express processes as nouns ("the activation of X", "expression", "regulation"). Example: "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow" → "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation". Apply in several places.
- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…"). Example: "These results show that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **This/these/they as subject: less than other fields** — target about 1.4 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: repeat the noun instead of this/it/they. Example: "X binds Y. This…" → "X binds Y. The binding of X to Y…". Apply in several places.

- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is less common in Neurology; future-work statements is more common in Neurology. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Neurology (use where it fits): "Future studies …"; "Previously we …"; "Here we …"; "Thus we …"; "Consistent with …"; "Our data …"; "…to the second…"

- Introduction (PMC11099104, Scientific Reports, 2024): "Over the past decade, studies on transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) have consistently demonstrated the utility of the concha and tragus, regions innervated by the auricular branch of the human vagus nerve, as valuable gateways for non-invasive brain stimulation . This emerging technique has garnered considerable attention for its potential in treating central nervous disorders, as well as for enhancing cognitive functions in healthy subjects . However, despite these promising outcomes, the precise mechanisms underlying these effects have not been completely elucidated."

Such a model should display phenotypes and cSVD risk factors that recapitulate the disease in humans, including hypertension and hypercholesterolemia. Our results revealed that LDLr−/−.Leiden mice fed a HFD and HFD+C exhibited cSVD characteristics, including reduced CBF, brain atrophy, impaired endothelial and BBB function, as well as vascular inflammation and neuroinflammation."
