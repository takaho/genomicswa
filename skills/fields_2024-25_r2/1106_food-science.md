# Food Science: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Food Science article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Food Science articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive.
- **Hedging verbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.4 per 300 words (all fields about 0.8 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose. Example: "This suggests that X causes Y" → "X causes Y" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: more than other fields** — target about 6.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make genes, proteins, cells, compounds, diseases or variables the grammatical subjects.
- **Modal hedges: less than other fields** — target about 0.6 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: may, might, could, would. Example: "X may explain Y" → "X explains Y" (only where the excerpt states it as a finding). Apply in several places.
- **Passive voice: more than other fields** — target about 2.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: report procedures and established facts in the passive ("X was observed", "Y has been reported").
- **Hedging adverbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.7 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often. Example: "X is likely involved" → "X is involved" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is more common in Food Science; patients/participants as subject is less common in Food Science. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Food Science (use where it fits): "The aim …"; "During the …"; "Moreover the …"; "In addition …"; "For the …"; "Therefore the …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Modal hedges: less than other fields** — target about 1.3 per 300 words (all fields about 2.0 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: may, might, could, would. Example: "X may explain Y" → "X explains Y" (only where the excerpt states it as a finding). Apply in several places.
- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: more than other fields** — target about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 5.7 in 10 clause subjects). How: make genes, proteins, cells, compounds, diseases or variables the grammatical subjects. Example: "We observed that X binds Y" → "X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 2.4 per 300 words (all fields about 1.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight. Example: "We found that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Word length: more than other fields** — target about 5.81 characters per word (all fields about 5.68 characters per word). How: prefer precise technical and Latinate terms and noun phrases. Example: "X goes up when Y is blocked" → "X increases upon inhibition of Y". Apply in several places.
- **Importance words: more than other fields** — target about 1.2 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major. Example: "X regulates Y" → "X is a key regulator of Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is more common in Food Science; patients/participants as subject is less common in Food Science; limitation statements is less common in Food Science. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Food Science (use where it fits): "Conclusion in …"; "Conclusion this …"; "The essential …"; "Overall the …"; "The other …"; "In particular …"
