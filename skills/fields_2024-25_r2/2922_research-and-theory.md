# Research and Theory: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Research and Theory article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Research and Theory articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive.
- **This/these/they as subject: more than other fields** — target about 1.9 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: refer back with this/these/they/which as subjects. Example: "X binds Y. The binding of X to Y…" → "X binds Y. This…". Apply in several places.
- **Vocabulary variety: less than other fields** — target MATTR about 0.70 (all fields MATTR about 0.72). How: repeat key terms consistently instead of synonyms. Example: keep one term for one concept: do not alternate "rise", "elevation", "increase". Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.7 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity. Example: "X binds Y" → "We found that X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Importance words: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use more of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major.
- **Passive voice: less than other fields** — target about 1.8 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: turn passives into active clauses with an explicit agent. Example: "X was measured in all samples" → "We measured X in all samples". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Research and Theory; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Research and Theory. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Research and Theory (use where it fits): "Therefore this …"; "A study …"; "In other …"; "Previous research …"; "This was …"; "As a …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **This/these/they as subject: more than other fields** — target about 1.9 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: refer back with this/these/they/which as subjects. Example: "X binds Y. The binding of X to Y…" → "X binds Y. This…". Apply in several places.
- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.3 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive.
- **Importance words: more than other fields** — target about 1.2 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major. Example: "X regulates Y" → "X is a key regulator of Y". Apply in several places.
- **Contrast connectives: less than other fields** — target about 1.0 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: however, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless, despite. Example: "However, X…" → "X…" where no contrast is meant. Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.3 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 5.7 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity. Example: "X binds Y" → "We found that X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Passive voice: less than other fields** — target about 1.7 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.0 in 10 clause subjects). How: turn passives into active clauses with an explicit agent. Example: "X was measured in all samples" → "We measured X in all samples". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Research and Theory; aim statements is more common in Research and Theory; limitation statements is more common in Research and Theory. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Research and Theory (use where it fits): "The findings …"; "Future research …"; "The study …"; "The results …"; "This can …"; "Limitations the …"

- Words often added by AI polishing (enhance, additionally, crucial, comprehensive, enhancing, insights, fostering, robust, foster, enhances) occur about 1.1 times per 300 words in Research and Theory; do not exceed that, and do not add them where the original has none.

Confucius once said, “Honest advice may be unpleasant, but it benefits actions,” and during the feudal era, institutions such as the Imperial Court of Censors were established, reflecting the cultural tradition of offering advice. With the rapid development of the global socioeconomic landscape and changes in organizational environments, the significance and scope of voice behavior have been continuously emphasized. Internationally, the concept of voice behavior originated from the EVI model proposed by American economist Hirschman [ ]."
- Discussion (PMC11587416, Nursing Reports, 2024): "The study’s findings highlight a critical issue in the management of coronary heart disease (CHD) among the Minangkabau ethnic group in West Sumatra, Indonesia. The research revealed that patients, their families, and even Indigenous leaders demonstrated inadequate knowledge about CHD and struggled to identify its early symptoms. This lack of understanding was consistent across all three groups, regardless of their education levels. Patients often misinterpreted CHD symptoms, attributing them to less serious conditions like gastritis or colds."
