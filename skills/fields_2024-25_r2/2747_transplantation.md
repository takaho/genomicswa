# Transplantation: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Transplantation article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Transplantation articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Contrast connectives: more than other fields** — target about 1.6 per 300 words (all fields about 1.2 per 300 words). How: use more of: however, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless, despite. Do not overshoot: stop at the target (at most about 2 per 300 words).
- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): more than other fields** — target about 22.0 per 300 words (all fields about 20.2 per 300 words). How: express processes as nouns ("the activation of X", "expression", "regulation"). Example: "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow" → "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation". Apply in several places.
- **Modal hedges: more than other fields** — target about 1.1 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: may, might, could, would. Example: "X explains Y" → "X may explain Y". Apply in several places.
- **This/these/they as subject: less than other fields** — target about 1.4 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: repeat the noun instead of this/it/they. Example: "X binds Y. This…" → "X binds Y. The binding of X to Y…". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Transplantation. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Transplantation (use where it fits): "The first …"; "The aim …"; "Therefore in …"; "The current …"; "In our …"; "Despite the …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Contrast connectives: more than other fields** — target about 1.7 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use more of: however, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless, despite.
- **Past tense: more than other fields** — target about 4.5 in 10 finite verbs (all fields about 4.1 in 10 finite verbs). How: report earlier studies and this study's observations in the past tense. Example: "Previous studies show that X regulates Y" → "Previous studies showed that X regulated Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Transplantation; patients/participants as subject is more common in Transplantation. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Transplantation (use where it fits): "In our …"; "Our study …"; "In a …"; "In conclusion …"; "Because of …"; "We did …"
