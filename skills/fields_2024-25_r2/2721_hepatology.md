# Hepatology: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Hepatology article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Hepatology articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.4 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight. Example: "We found that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **This/these/they as subject: less than other fields** — target about 1.3 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: repeat the noun instead of this/it/they. Example: "X binds Y. This…" → "X binds Y. The binding of X to Y…". Apply in several places.
- **Summary connectives: more than other fields** — target about 0.2 per 300 words (all fields about 0.2 per 300 words). How: use more of: together, collectively, taken together, overall, in summary, in conclusion. Example: "…" → "Taken together, …". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Hepatology. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Hepatology (use where it fits): "Recent studies …"; "Nevertheless the …"; "The aim …"; "However the …"; "However there …"; "Moreover the …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 2.2 per 300 words (all fields about 1.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight.
- **Hedging adverbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.8 per 300 words (all fields about 1.0 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often. Example: "X is likely involved" → "X is involved" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- **Findings/data/study as subject: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.3 in 10 clause subjects). How: make findings/data/results/this study the subject ("These findings suggest…").
- **This/these/they as subject: less than other fields** — target about 1.4 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: repeat the noun instead of this/it/they. Example: "X binds Y. This…" → "X binds Y. The binding of X to Y…". Apply in several places.

Typical discussion phrasing in Hepatology (use where it fits): "Therefore we …"; "Our study …"; "In conclusion …"; "In our …"; "Second we …"; "However its …"

## Vocabulary
- Words often added by AI polishing (additionally, notably, crucial, comprehensive, enhance, insights, robust, enhancing, pivotal, elucidate) occur about 1.0 times per 300 words in Hepatology; do not exceed that, and do not add them where the original has none.

The screening approach is characterized by its dual-phase structure.
