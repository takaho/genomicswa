# Emergency Medicine: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Emergency Medicine article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Emergency Medicine articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Word length: less than other fields** — target about 5.69 characters per word (all fields about 5.81 characters per word). How: prefer short plain words and verbs. Example: "X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X goes up when Y is blocked". Apply in several places.
- **Additive connectives: less than other fields** — target about 0.9 per 300 words (all fields about 1.2 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: moreover, furthermore, in addition, also, similarly, likewise. Do not overshoot: stop at the target (at most about 1 per 300 words).
- **Booster verbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.9 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight. Example: "These results highlight/reveal that X…" → "These results indicate that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 6.0 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity. Example: "X binds Y" → "We found that X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): less than other fields** — target about 18.8 per 300 words (all fields about 20.2 per 300 words). How: express processes as verbs ("X activates Y"). Example: "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation" → "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow". Apply in several places.
- **"It is…"/"There are…" constructions: more than other fields** — target about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: use "It is … that" and "There is/are …" constructions. Example: "X is known to regulate Y" → "It is known that X regulates Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Emergency Medicine; aim statements is more common in Emergency Medicine; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Emergency Medicine. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Emergency Medicine (use where it fits): "This study …"; "During the …"; "The aim …"; "Therefore this …"; "In a …"; "As such …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

Notably, 97.06% of patients (33/34) in the ERAT group achieved complete pain relief within 2 days of treatment, a significantly higher rate than that observed in the LA group (77.94%, 53/68). This finding highlighted the ability of direct visualization ERAT to effectively and rapidly alleviate pain symptoms."
