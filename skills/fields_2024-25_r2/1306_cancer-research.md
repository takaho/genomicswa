# Cancer Research: research-article prose style, 2024–2025

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Cancer Research article of 2024–2025. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Cancer Research articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight. Example: "We found that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…"). Example: "These results show that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Importance words: more than other fields** — target about 1.4 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use more of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major. Example: "X regulates Y" → "X is a key regulator of Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): gap/novelty statements is more common in Cancer Research. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Cancer Research (use where it fits): "In this …"; "We present …"; "Research has …"; "In our …"; "However the …"; "It has …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…"). Do not overshoot: stop at the target.
- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 2.3 per 300 words (all fields about 1.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight. Example: "We found that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging adverbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.8 per 300 words (all fields about 1.0 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often. Example: "X is likely involved" → "X is involved" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- **Additive connectives: more than other fields** — target about 1.7 per 300 words (all fields about 1.5 per 300 words). How: use more of: moreover, furthermore, in addition, also, similarly, likewise.
- **This/these/they as subject: less than other fields** — target about 1.3 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: repeat the noun instead of this/it/they. Example: "X binds Y. This…" → "X binds Y. The binding of X to Y…". Apply in several places.
- **Importance words: more than other fields** — target about 1.1 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major. Example: "X regulates Y" → "X is a key regulator of Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): gap/novelty statements is more common in Cancer Research; patients/participants as subject is less common in Cancer Research; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Cancer Research. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Cancer Research (use where it fits): "Here we …"; "The role …"; "To further …"; "Moreover a …"; "In summary …"; "To investigate …"

## Style illustration (Cancer Research, 2024–2025; verbatim, CC BY; do not copy content)
- Introduction (PMC11152612, Computational and Structural Biotechnology Journal, 2024): "Today, cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide and presents a significant challenge for the healthcare system . To uncover the critical genomic and genetic changes in cancer, it is essential to explore various aspects, including genomic alterations, methylation patterns, and changes in mRNA and microRNA (miRNA) expression levels. MiRNAs play a crucial role in carcinogenesis by binding to mRNAs and consequently influencing the protein profile of the cell ."
- Discussion (PMC12472857, Pharmaceuticals, 2025): "Recently, several studies have identified the important role of miR-25-3p in various cancers; however, the specific role of this miRNA in UBC and its associated mechanisms remain unclear. A KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the predicted target genes for miR-25-3p highlighted key pathways related to bladder cancer and platinum resistance, emphasizing the importance of this miRNA in the context of UBC. GO demonstrated its essential role in cancer-related cellular pathways, including cell migration, proliferation, adhesion, and oxidative stress."
