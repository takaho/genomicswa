# Aquatic Science: research-article prose style, 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Aquatic Science article of 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Aquatic Science articles (2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): less than other fields** — target about 17.0 per 300 words (all fields about 19.0 per 300 words). How: express processes as verbs ("X activates Y"). Example: "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation" → "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow". Apply in several places.
- **Importance words: more than other fields** — target about 1.3 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major. Example: "X regulates Y" → "X is a key regulator of Y". Apply in several places.
- **Vocabulary variety: more than other fields** — target MATTR about 0.71 (all fields MATTR about 0.70). How: vary wording; avoid repeating the same noun or verb in neighbouring sentences. Example: avoid repeating "increase" in neighbouring sentences: "rise", "elevation", "higher levels". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging verbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.6 per 300 words (all fields about 0.8 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose. Example: "This suggests that X causes Y" → "X causes Y" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: more than other fields** — target about 6.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make genes, proteins, cells, compounds, diseases or variables the grammatical subjects. Example: "We observed that X binds Y" → "X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Booster verbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.8 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish. Example: "These results highlight/reveal that X…" → "These results indicate that X…". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is less common in Aquatic Science; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is more common in Aquatic Science. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Aquatic Science (use where it fits): "The present …"; "In previous …"; "Moreover the …"; "Similarly in …"; "At present …"; "Hence the …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: more than other fields** — target about 6.4 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 5.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: make genes, proteins, cells, compounds, diseases or variables the grammatical subjects.
- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive.
- **Sentence length: more than other fields** — target about 27 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: join closely related short sentences with subordinate clauses (which, whereas, because). Example: "X was measured. It increased with age." → "X, which was measured in all samples, increased with age.". Apply in several places.
- **"It is…"/"There are…" constructions: less than other fields** — target about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: replace "It is known that…"/"There are…" with the entity as subject. Example: "It is known that X regulates Y" → "X regulates Y". Apply in several places.
- **Findings/data/study as subject: less than other fields** — target about 1.0 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make the entity or "we" the subject instead of data/results/study. Example: "These data indicate that X binds Y" → "X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): less than other fields** — target about 17.7 per 300 words (all fields about 18.9 per 300 words). How: express processes as verbs ("X activates Y"). Example: "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation" → "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): limitation statements is less common in Aquatic Science; patients/participants as subject is less common in Aquatic Science; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is more common in Aquatic Science. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Aquatic Science (use where it fits): "Our results …"; "This result …"; "These results …"; "Similarly the …"; "Similar results …"; "The present …"

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Aquatic Science articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.3 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Restore explicit causal links where the reasoning is causal: "therefore", "thus", "because" (these were about one third more frequent before 2023).
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- Keep established methods and findings in the passive where the field used it ("X was measured", "Y has been reported").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Aquatic Science introduction targets for these habits: causal connectives about 1.0 per 300 words; importance words about 1.3 per 300 words; nominalisations about 17.0 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.3 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.7 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.68 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Aquatic Science discussion targets for these habits: causal connectives about 0.9 per 300 words; importance words about 0.8 per 300 words; nominalisations about 17.7 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 1.0 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.4 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.50 characters per word.
