# Genetics: research-article prose style, 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Genetics article of 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Genetics articles (2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…"). Do not overshoot: stop at the target.
- **Word length: less than other fields** — target about 5.60 characters per word (all fields about 5.66 characters per word). How: prefer short plain words and verbs. Example: "X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X goes up when Y is blocked". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is less common in Genetics. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Genetics (use where it fits): "Here we …"; "We then …"; "With the …"; "In contrast …"; "We show …"; "Finally we …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…").
- **Findings/data/study as subject: less than other fields** — target about 1.0 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make the entity or "we" the subject instead of data/results/study. Example: "These data indicate that X binds Y" → "X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Past tense: less than other fields** — target about 3.8 in 10 finite verbs (all fields about 4.4 in 10 finite verbs). How: use the present tense for established knowledge and interpretation. Example: "Previous studies showed that X regulated Y" → "Previous studies show that X regulates Y". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use more of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose. Example: "X causes Y" → "These data suggest that X causes Y". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging adverbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.2 per 300 words (all fields about 1.0 per 300 words). How: use more of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often. Example: "X is involved" → "X is likely involved". Apply in several places.
- **Sentence length: more than other fields** — target about 26 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: join closely related short sentences with subordinate clauses (which, whereas, because). Example: "X was measured. It increased with age." → "X, which was measured in all samples, increased with age.". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): limitation statements is less common in Genetics; patients/participants as subject is less common in Genetics. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Genetics (use where it fits): "Here we …"; "For the …"; "In contrast …"; "Also we …"; "We therefore …"; "We have …"

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Genetics articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.3 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Restore explicit causal links where the reasoning is causal: "therefore", "thus", "because" (these were about one third more frequent before 2023).
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- Keep established methods and findings in the passive where the field used it ("X was measured", "Y has been reported").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Genetics introduction targets for these habits: causal connectives about 0.8 per 300 words; importance words about 0.9 per 300 words; nominalisations about 19.4 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.6 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.60 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Genetics discussion targets for these habits: causal connectives about 0.9 per 300 words; importance words about 0.8 per 300 words; nominalisations about 18.7 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 1.0 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.3 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.45 characters per word.

## Style illustration (Genetics, 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants); verbatim, CC BY; do not copy content)

Vancomycin is one of a number of antibiotics that target Lipid II rather than the enzymes that use Lipid II as a substrate . Inducible high-level resistance to vancomycin is commonly found in soil bacteria including the Actinomycetes where it has been genome-encoded for millennia ."
- Discussion (PMC6363789, Scientific Reports, 2019): "In the last few years, the optimization of the TRS have been addressed in several studies . These studies have helped researchers better understand the factors affecting TRS optimization and core subset selection. In this paper, we compared the performance of four optimization methods under two scenarios (U-Opt and T-Opt) on two datasets and 13 traits. In addition, we compare several designs of experiment strategies to define an optimal TRS using a genetic algorithm. Our results indicated that the optimization approach showed better predictions on average than did random sampling."
