# Physiology: research-article prose style, 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Physiology article of 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Physiology articles (2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Importance words: more than other fields** — target about 1.3 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major. Example: "X regulates Y" → "X is a key regulator of Y". Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: more than other fields** — target about 6.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make genes, proteins, cells, compounds, diseases or variables the grammatical subjects. Example: "We observed that X binds Y" → "X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Word length: more than other fields** — target about 5.74 characters per word (all fields about 5.66 characters per word). How: prefer precise technical and Latinate terms and noun phrases. Example: "X goes up when Y is blocked" → "X increases upon inhibition of Y". Apply in several places.
- **Passive voice: more than other fields** — target about 2.8 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: report procedures and established facts in the passive ("X was observed", "Y has been reported"). Example: "We measured X in all samples" → "X was measured in all samples". Apply in several places.
- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.3 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish. Example: "We found that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging adverbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.7 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often. Example: "X is likely involved" → "X is involved" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is less common in Physiology; aim statements is less common in Physiology; method/model/approach as subject is less common in Physiology. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Physiology (use where it fits): "We have …"; "We recently …"; "The a …"; "These results …"; "We found …"; "Our results …"; "…essential roles in…"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: more than other fields** — target about 6.1 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 5.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: make genes, proteins, cells, compounds, diseases or variables the grammatical subjects.
- **Hedging verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.6 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use more of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose. Example: "X causes Y" → "These data suggest that X causes Y". Apply in several places.
- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 2.0 per 300 words (all fields about 1.7 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish. Example: "We found that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Importance words: more than other fields** — target about 0.9 per 300 words (all fields about 0.7 per 300 words). How: use more of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major.
- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…"). Example: "These results show that X…" → "We show that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Word length: more than other fields** — target about 5.55 characters per word (all fields about 5.49 characters per word). How: prefer precise technical and Latinate terms and noun phrases. Example: "X goes up when Y is blocked" → "X increases upon inhibition of Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is less common in Physiology; limitation statements is less common in Physiology. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Physiology (use where it fits): "Taken together …"; "Here we …"; "Our data …"; "Moreover we …"; "These data …"; "These results …"

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Physiology articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.3 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Restore explicit causal links where the reasoning is causal: "therefore", "thus", "because" (these were about one third more frequent before 2023).
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- Keep established methods and findings in the passive where the field used it ("X was measured", "Y has been reported").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Physiology introduction targets for these habits: causal connectives about 0.8 per 300 words; importance words about 1.3 per 300 words; nominalisations about 18.6 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.8 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.74 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Physiology discussion targets for these habits: causal connectives about 0.8 per 300 words; importance words about 0.9 per 300 words; nominalisations about 18.9 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 1.1 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.4 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.55 characters per word.
