# Research and Theory: research-article prose style, 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Research and Theory article of 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Research and Theory articles (2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Vocabulary variety: less than other fields** — target MATTR about 0.68 (all fields MATTR about 0.70). How: repeat key terms consistently instead of synonyms. Example: keep one term for one concept: do not alternate "rise", "elevation", "increase". Apply in several places.
- **Passive voice: less than other fields** — target about 2.1 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: turn passives into active clauses with an explicit agent. Example: "X was measured in all samples" → "We measured X in all samples". Apply in several places.
- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive.
- **This/these/they as subject: more than other fields** — target about 1.9 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: refer back with this/these/they/which as subjects. Example: "X binds Y. The binding of X to Y…" → "X binds Y. This…". Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.7 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity. Example: "X binds Y" → "We found that X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Booster verbs: less than other fields** — target about 0.7 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish. Example: "These results highlight/reveal that X…" → "These results indicate that X…". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Research and Theory; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Research and Theory. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Research and Theory (use where it fits): "A study …"; "This paper …"; "It also …"; "In a …"; "Therefore this …"; "The research …"

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Research and Theory articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.4 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Restore explicit causal links where the reasoning is causal: "therefore", "thus", "because" (these were about one third more frequent before 2023).
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- Keep established methods and findings in the passive where the field used it ("X was measured", "Y has been reported").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Research and Theory introduction targets for these habits: causal connectives about 1.0 per 300 words; importance words about 1.2 per 300 words; nominalisations about 20.1 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.1 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.58 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Research and Theory discussion targets for these habits: causal connectives about 1.0 per 300 words; importance words about 0.9 per 300 words; nominalisations about 19.9 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 1.1 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.0 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.50 characters per word.
