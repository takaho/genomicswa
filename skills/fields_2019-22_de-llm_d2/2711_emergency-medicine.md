# Emergency Medicine: research-article prose style, 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Emergency Medicine article of 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Emergency Medicine articles (2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Modal hedges: more than other fields** — target about 1.4 per 300 words (all fields about 1.0 per 300 words). How: use more of: may, might, could, would. Example: "X explains Y" → "X may explain Y". Apply in several places.
- **Word length: less than other fields** — target about 5.56 characters per word (all fields about 5.66 characters per word). How: prefer short plain words and verbs. Example: "X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X goes up when Y is blocked". Apply in several places.
- **Additive connectives: less than other fields** — target about 0.9 per 300 words (all fields about 1.2 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: moreover, furthermore, in addition, also, similarly, likewise. Do not overshoot: stop at the target (at most about 1 per 300 words).
- **Past tense: more than other fields** — target about 2.7 in 10 finite verbs (all fields about 2.3 in 10 finite verbs). How: report earlier studies and this study's observations in the past tense. Example: "Previous studies show that X regulates Y" → "Previous studies showed that X regulated Y". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging adverbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.1 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often. Example: "X is involved" → "X is likely involved". Apply in several places.
- **"It is…"/"There are…" constructions: more than other fields** — target about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: use "It is … that" and "There is/are …" constructions. Example: "X is known to regulate Y" → "It is known that X regulates Y". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Emergency Medicine; aim statements is more common in Emergency Medicine; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Emergency Medicine. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Emergency Medicine (use where it fits): "The aim …"; "A study …"; "The number …"; "We aimed …"; "Several studies …"; "This may …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Booster verbs: less than other fields** — target about 1.3 per 300 words (all fields about 1.7 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish. Example: "These results highlight/reveal that X…" → "These results indicate that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging adverbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.3 per 300 words (all fields about 1.0 per 300 words). How: use more of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often. Example: "X is involved" → "X is likely involved". Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.3 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 5.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity. Example: "X binds Y" → "We found that X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Modal hedges: more than other fields** — target about 2.4 per 300 words (all fields about 2.0 per 300 words). How: use more of: may, might, could, would. Example: "X explains Y" → "X may explain Y". Apply in several places.
- **Sentence length: less than other fields** — target about 24 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: split long sentences; one idea per sentence. Example: "X, which was measured in all samples and which increased with age, was associated with Y." → "X was measured in all samples. It increased with age and was associated with Y.". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging verbs: less than other fields** — target about 1.0 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose. Example: "This suggests that X causes Y" → "X causes Y" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Emergency Medicine; limitation statements is more common in Emergency Medicine; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Emergency Medicine. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Emergency Medicine (use where it fits): "A study …"; "Our study …"; "This study …"; "In a …"; "During the …"; "The study …"

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Emergency Medicine articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.2 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Restore explicit causal links where the reasoning is causal: "therefore", "thus", "because" (these were about one third more frequent before 2023).
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- Keep established methods and findings in the passive where the field used it ("X was measured", "Y has been reported").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Emergency Medicine introduction targets for these habits: causal connectives about 0.9 per 300 words; importance words about 1.0 per 300 words; nominalisations about 18.5 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.4 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.56 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Emergency Medicine discussion targets for these habits: causal connectives about 0.9 per 300 words; importance words about 0.8 per 300 words; nominalisations about 18.0 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 1.2 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.1 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.42 characters per word.
