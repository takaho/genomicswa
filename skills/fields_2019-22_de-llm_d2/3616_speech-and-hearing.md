# Speech and Hearing: research-article prose style, 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Speech and Hearing article of 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Speech and Hearing articles (2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Modal hedges: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.0 per 300 words). How: use more of: may, might, could, would. Do not overshoot: stop at the target (at most about 2 per 300 words).
- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): more than other fields** — target about 20.9 per 300 words (all fields about 19.0 per 300 words). How: express processes as nouns ("the activation of X", "expression", "regulation"). Example: "Y activates X, which changes how cells grow" → "Activation of X by Y alters cell proliferation". Apply in several places.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.8 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity. Example: "X binds Y" → "We found that X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **Vocabulary variety: less than other fields** — target MATTR about 0.69 (all fields MATTR about 0.70). How: repeat key terms consistently instead of synonyms. Example: keep one term for one concept: do not alternate "rise", "elevation", "increase". Apply in several places.
- **Findings/data/study as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.9 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects). How: make findings/data/results/this study the subject ("These findings suggest…"). Example: "X increased, suggesting…" → "These findings suggest that X…". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging adverbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.0 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often. Example: "X is involved" → "X is likely involved". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Speech and Hearing; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Speech and Hearing. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Speech and Hearing (use where it fits): "The current …"; "For the …"; "That is …"; "This finding …"; "It can …"; "This is …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.3 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 5.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity. Example: "X binds Y" → "We found that X binds Y". Apply in several places.
- **"It is…"/"There are…" constructions: more than other fields** — target about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: use "It is … that" and "There is/are …" constructions. Example: "X is known to regulate Y" → "It is known that X regulates Y". Apply in several places.
- **Modal hedges: more than other fields** — target about 2.3 per 300 words (all fields about 2.0 per 300 words). How: use more of: may, might, could, would.
- **"We" as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects). How: avoid "we"; make the finding, entity or method the subject, or use the passive. Example: "We found that X increased" → "X increased". Apply in several places.
- **Importance words: less than other fields** — target about 0.6 per 300 words (all fields about 0.7 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major.
- Context only (do not force): patients/participants as subject is more common in Speech and Hearing; future-work statements is more common in Speech and Hearing; organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject is less common in Speech and Hearing. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Speech and Hearing articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.2 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Restore explicit causal links where the reasoning is causal: "therefore", "thus", "because" (these were about one third more frequent before 2023).
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- Keep established methods and findings in the passive where the field used it ("X was measured", "Y has been reported").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Speech and Hearing introduction targets for these habits: causal connectives about 0.9 per 300 words; importance words about 0.8 per 300 words; nominalisations about 20.9 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.3 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.9 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.5 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.64 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Speech and Hearing discussion targets for these habits: causal connectives about 1.0 per 300 words; importance words about 0.6 per 300 words; nominalisations about 19.8 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 1.3 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.2 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.49 characters per word.
