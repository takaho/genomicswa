# Surgery: research-article prose style, 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a Surgery article of 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants). Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: open-access Surgery articles (2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants)) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average.

## General rules (learned from earlier rewriting runs)
- Change only the features listed below, in the direction and by the amount shown. Leave everything else as in the original.
- For features marked "Apply in several places", earlier rewrites changed too little: count the feature in the original and change it throughout the excerpt, not just once.
- Do not shorten sentences, split sentences, or turn passive clauses into active ones unless a target below asks for it; otherwise keep the original sentence length and voice.
- Do not change who or what the sentences are about (patients, animals, plants, methods): that follows from the subject matter.
- Do not add aims, limitations, novelty claims or future-work statements that the original does not contain.

## Introduction: what to change (most important first)

- **Contrast connectives: more than other fields** — target about 1.4 per 300 words (all fields about 1.2 per 300 words). How: use more of: however, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless, despite.
- Context only (do not force): aim statements is more common in Surgery. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical introduction phrasing in Surgery (use where it fits): "The aim …"; "We present …"; "If the …"; "There were …"; "Recently it …"; "Although the …"

## Discussion: what to change (most important first)

- **Sentence length: less than other fields** — target about 24 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: split long sentences; one idea per sentence. Example: "X, which was measured in all samples and which increased with age, was associated with Y." → "X was measured in all samples. It increased with age and was associated with Y.". Apply in several places.
- **Hedging verbs: less than other fields** — target about 1.0 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose. Example: "This suggests that X causes Y" → "X causes Y" (only where stated as a finding). Apply in several places.
- **Contrast connectives: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use more of: however, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless, despite. Example: "X… Y…" → "X…; however, Y…". Apply in several places.
- Context only (do not force): limitation statements is more common in Surgery; patients/participants as subject is more common in Surgery. Where the original already contains such content, phrase it in the field's way.

Typical discussion phrasing in Surgery (use where it fits): "In our …"; "In their …"; "First this …"; "There was …"; "First it …"; "However a …"

## Remove AI-polishing style
Recent text often carries vocabulary and habits that became common after 2023. In Surgery articles of 2019–2022:
- Words such as additionally, notably, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, insights, landscape, interplay, enhance, elucidate, underscore, showcase, foster, harness, delve, intricate, multifaceted occurred about 0.3 times per 300 words in total. Replace them with plain wording, for example: additionally → in addition / also; notably → (delete) / in particular; crucial / pivotal → important / key, or state the role; robust → strong / consistent; insights → information / understanding; enhance → increase / improve; elucidate → determine / define; underscore / highlight → indicate / show; landscape → profile / range; interplay → interaction.
- Restore explicit causal links where the reasoning is causal: "therefore", "thus", "because" (these were about one third more frequent before 2023).
- Prefer verbs to abstract nouns and plain words to long Latinate ones ("X increases upon inhibition of Y" → "X increases when Y is inhibited").
- Use "we" for what the authors did and found, rather than "This study/These findings" as subjects.
- Keep established methods and findings in the passive where the field used it ("X was measured", "Y has been reported").
- Avoid stacking importance words (crucial, key, essential, pivotal) and "highlight/underscore/reveal".
- 2019–2022 Surgery introduction targets for these habits: causal connectives about 1.0 per 300 words; importance words about 0.8 per 300 words; nominalisations about 19.2 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.5 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.66 characters per word.
- 2019–2022 Surgery discussion targets for these habits: causal connectives about 0.9 per 300 words; importance words about 0.6 per 300 words; nominalisations about 18.1 per 300 words; "we" as subject about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects; findings/data/study as subject about 1.2 in 10 clause subjects; passive about 2.3 in 10 clause subjects; word length about 5.47 characters per word.

## Style illustration (Surgery, 2019–2022 (before AI writing assistants); verbatim, CC BY; do not copy content)
- Introduction (PMC7453789, SICOT-J, 2020): "Historically, for Magerl, type A fracture was a strictly bony and stable injury [ ]. Nonetheless, several studies have highlighted a loss of sagittal correction after fracture treatment, whether it was conservative or surgical [ ]. This loss of correction was evaluated around 10% and was located in the disc for 75% and in the vertebral body for the remaining 25%. However, most of these later measurements were performed on plain radiographies which have been correlated to a lower accuracy than the measurements performed with CT-scans."
- Discussion (PMC6745112, Critical Care Research and Practice, 2019): "An OPTX is defined as a PTX seen by CT that is not seen on CXR [ ]. Implicit to this definition is that the OPTX is a small volume of extrapleural air, but there is no defined volume. Carr et al. demonstrated that upto 200 cc extrapleural air could be present without being seen by supine CXR [ ]. The definitive management of OPTX remains an ongoing debate. Previous studies have reported that 48%–63.3% of OPTX cases can be managed conservatively without chest tube placement [ ]."
