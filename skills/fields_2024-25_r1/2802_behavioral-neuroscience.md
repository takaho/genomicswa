# Behavioral Neuroscience: research-article prose style (2024–2025)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a current Behavioral Neuroscience article. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: 300 open-access Behavioral Neuroscience articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average for contrast.

## Introduction: what to change

- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…").
- **Hedging verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.2 per 300 words (all fields about 0.8 per 300 words). How: use more of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose.
- **Modal hedges: more than other fields** — target about 1.4 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: may, might, could, would.
- **Organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.1 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.1 in 10 clause subjects). How: make the organism (mice, plants, strains) the subject where the content allows.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.9 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity.

Typical introduction phrasing in Behavioral Neuroscience (use where it fits the content): "Consistent with …"; "That is …"; "We previously …"; "These findings …"; "As such …"; "Taken together …"; "First we …"; "Second we …"
