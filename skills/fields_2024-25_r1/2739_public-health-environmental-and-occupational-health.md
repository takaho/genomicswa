# Public Health, Environmental and Occupational Health: research-article prose style (2024–2025)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a current Public Health, Environmental and Occupational Health article. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: 300 open-access Public Health, Environmental and Occupational Health articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average for contrast.

## Introduction: what to change

- **Patients/participants as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make patients/participants/individuals the subject where the content allows.
- **Sentence length: more than other fields** — target about 26 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: join closely related short sentences with subordinate clauses (which, whereas, because).
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.9 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 6.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity.
- **Passive voice: less than other fields** — target about 1.9 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: turn passives into active clauses with an explicit agent.
- **Past tense: more than other fields** — target about 2.3 in 10 finite verbs (all fields about 2.0 in 10 finite verbs). How: report earlier studies and this study's observations in the past tense.

Typical introduction phrasing in Public Health, Environmental and Occupational Health (use where it fits the content): "Despite the …"; "Similarly a …"; "Since the …"; "More than …"; "To the …"; "There is …"; "Despite this …"; "Most of …"

## Discussion: what to change

- **Patients/participants as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.6 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.3 in 10 clause subjects). How: make patients/participants/individuals the subject where the content allows.
- **This/these/they as subject: more than other fields** — target about 1.8 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: refer back with this/these/they/which as subjects.
- **Biological/clinical entities as subject: less than other fields** — target about 5.1 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 5.7 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we", the study or the finding the subject instead of the entity.
- **Limitation statements: more than other fields** — target about 0.5 per 300 words (all fields about 0.3 per 300 words). How: where the excerpt mentions constraints, name them as limitations: "A limitation of…", "should be interpreted with caution", "cannot rule out", "may not generalize".
- **Modal hedges: more than other fields** — target about 2.3 per 300 words (all fields about 2.0 per 300 words). How: use more of: may, might, could, would.
- **Passive voice: less than other fields** — target about 1.8 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 2.0 in 10 clause subjects). How: turn passives into active clauses with an explicit agent.
- **Hedging adverbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.2 per 300 words (all fields about 1.0 per 300 words). How: use more of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often.

Typical discussion phrasing in Public Health, Environmental and Occupational Health (use where it fits the content): "The findings …"; "This study …"; "This finding …"; "It may …"; "However previous …"; "Limitations this …"; "In line …"; "We were …"

If managers do not observe practice changes or trust the reporting process, health professionals may experience similar doubts. Previous studies have also shown that health professionals who report incidents often have negative experiences regarding the management of incident reports (Woo and Avery ). Negative experiences, such as being blamed, naturally decrease health professionals' willingness to report."

## Procedure

2. Rewrite sentence by sentence, moving each of those features toward its target and leaving the rest unchanged.
3. Check that every number, [REF] marker, abbreviation and technical term is still present and that no claim was added or removed.
