# Pathology and Forensic Medicine: research-article prose style (2024–2025)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a current Pathology and Forensic Medicine article. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: 300 open-access Pathology and Forensic Medicine articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average for contrast.

## Introduction: what to change

- **Nominalisations (-tion, -ment, -ity…): less than other fields** — target about 17.7 per 300 words (all fields about 20.2 per 300 words). How: express processes as verbs ("X activates Y").
- **Summary connectives: more than other fields** — target about 0.3 per 300 words (all fields about 0.2 per 300 words). How: use more of: together, collectively, taken together, overall, in summary, in conclusion.
- **Hedging adverbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.2 per 300 words (all fields about 0.9 per 300 words). How: use more of: likely, possibly, probably, potentially, generally, relatively, largely, often.
- **Importance words: less than other fields** — target about 0.8 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use fewer of: critical, crucial, key, essential, important(ly), fundamental, vital, major.
- **Word length: less than other fields** — target about 5.73 characters per word (all fields about 5.81 characters per word). How: prefer short plain words and verbs.
- **Organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.0 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.1 in 10 clause subjects). How: make the gene, molecule or process the subject rather than the organism.
- **This/these/they as subject: less than other fields** — target about 1.3 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 1.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: repeat the noun instead of this/it/they.

Typical introduction phrasing in Pathology and Forensic Medicine (use where it fits the content): "In this …"; "However no …"; "Although the …"; "In our …"; "Therefore in …"; "The aim …"; "However there …"; "More recently …"
