# Cellular and Molecular Neuroscience: research-article prose style (2024–2025)

Use this to rewrite Introduction or Discussion prose so that it reads like a current Cellular and Molecular Neuroscience article. Change style only: never change numbers, statistics, citations, abbreviations, technical terms, claims or the subject matter.

Evidence: 300 open-access Cellular and Molecular Neuroscience articles (2024–2025) compared with 90 life-science fields. Targets are this field's averages; "all fields" gives the cross-field average for contrast.

## Introduction: what to change

- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.7 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.4 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…").
- **Booster verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.5 per 300 words (all fields about 1.1 per 300 words). How: use more of: show, demonstrate, reveal, confirm, establish, highlight.
- **Patients/participants as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.1 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.2 in 10 clause subjects). How: foreground the biology or variable rather than patients/participants.
- **Gap/novelty statements: more than other fields** — target about 0.4 per 300 words (all fields about 0.3 per 300 words). How: where the excerpt implies a gap, state it with: "remains unclear", "little is known", "poorly understood", "novel", "for the first time".

Typical introduction phrasing in Cellular and Molecular Neuroscience (use where it fits the content): "Here we …"; "We found …"; "These findings …"; "Our findings …"; "This suggests …"; "We then …"; "In this …"; "These results …"

## Discussion: what to change

- **"We" as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.8 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.5 in 10 clause subjects). How: make "we" the subject of what the study did or found ("We found…", "Here, we show…").
- **Patients/participants as subject: less than other fields** — target about 0.1 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.3 in 10 clause subjects). How: foreground the biology or variable rather than patients/participants.
- **Past tense: less than other fields** — target about 3.4 in 10 finite verbs (all fields about 4.1 in 10 finite verbs). How: use the present tense for established knowledge and interpretation.
- **Organisms (mice, plants, strains) as subject: more than other fields** — target about 0.1 in 10 clause subjects (all fields about 0.1 in 10 clause subjects). How: make the organism (mice, plants, strains) the subject where the content allows.
- **Hedging verbs: more than other fields** — target about 1.6 per 300 words (all fields about 1.3 per 300 words). How: use more of: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, propose.
- **Limitation statements: less than other fields** — target about 0.2 per 300 words (all fields about 0.3 per 300 words). How: avoid explicit limitation formulas: "A limitation of…", "should be interpreted with caution", "cannot rule out", "may not generalize".
- **Sentence length: more than other fields** — target about 26 words per sentence (all fields about 25 words per sentence). How: join closely related short sentences with subordinate clauses (which, whereas, because).

Typical discussion phrasing in Cellular and Molecular Neuroscience (use where it fits the content): "Here we …"; "Our data …"; "Consistent with …"; "We found …"; "We show …"; "Together these …"; "We demonstrate …"; "Taken together …"

3. Check that every number, [REF] marker, abbreviation and technical term is still present and that no claim was added or removed.
